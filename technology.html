BEHIND THE HOMEPAGE EXPERIENCE

How did the words
get there?

The system learns patterns in recorded neural activity. It uses those patterns to predict speech, then turns the prediction into words.

Where were the recording arrays?

In the Stanford T12 study, four intracortical arrays recorded from two areas on the left side of the cortex: two arrays in ventral premotor cortex (area 6v), and two in area 44, part of Broca’s area. The paper’s subsequent speech-decoding analyses used 6v recordings.

Our animation therefore sends illustrative signals from the two 6v sites. Area 44 is shown as another recorded region, not a second source for this decoding path. Dorsal and ventral describe the relative positions of the 6v arrays; they do not mean the back of the head.

The model is a generic head scan without the participant’s MRI or cortical surface. Markers indicate approximate regional relationships, are enlarged for visibility, and are not a precise implantation map. The translucent surface is a visual aid, not a reconstruction of exposed cortex. Refer to Figure 1 and Extended Data Figures 2 and 7 in the original paper for the study’s anatomy.

These sites describe Stanford’s study. We have not established that Neuralink’s speech-restoration system uses the same coordinates, array design or surgical placement. See Neuralink’s own speech-restoration information for that separate programme.

BEFORE THE DEMONSTRATION · TRAINING

The model first needs examples.

During training, the participant attempts prompted speech while electrodes record neural activity. The researchers use recordings and speech labels to train a decoder to associate patterns of activity with speech sounds. This mapping is learned from a participant’s data; it is not a universal dictionary of thoughts.

Follow one prediction.

  1. ∿ ∿01 · RECORD

    Neural features

    Electrodes record electrical activity. Processing turns the recordings into numerical features over time.

  2. ● · ●02 · PREDICT

    Speech sounds

    The recurrent neural network predicts probabilities for speech-sound units called phonemes.

  3. Aa03 · DECODE

    Words in context

    A language-model decoder combines sound predictions with language patterns to find likely words. Its partial prediction can change.

  4. “ ”04 · OUTPUT

    Text, then voice

    The final prediction becomes text. Our website can read that text with a standard synthetic voice.

Conceptual diagram drawn for this guide. The study’s code separates the neural decoder and language-model decoder. The homepage replays recorded results; it does not run these models.

Why did the words change?

A partial prediction uses the information available so far. Later activity can support a different sequence, so the system may revise its words. The final result can also differ from the last partial prediction.

Where does the voice come from?

On this website, your browser reads the decoded text. It is an extra output step, separate from neural decoding. It does not reproduce the participant’s voice.

And where does Neuralink fit?

Neuralink sparked the project’s subject. The Stanford recordings, the scanned head and the illustrated device in this website are separate sources. The demonstration explains a general speech BCI process and does not show Neuralink hardware performance.
